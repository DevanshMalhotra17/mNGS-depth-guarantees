\begin{abstract}
\small
Clinical metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) enables rapid
identification of infectious agents by matching sequenced DNA reads from
patient samples against a curated reference database. This approach is
particularly valuable in time-critical settings where conventional
culture-based diagnosis is too slow, yet it is most likely to fail in
precisely the scenarios of greatest clinical concern: when a pathogen has
diverged substantially from any entry in the reference database, as occurs
with emerging variants, cross-species spillovers, and novel outbreak
strains. In the absence of a principled mathematical framework, the minimum
sequencing depth required to guarantee detection of a divergent pathogen
remains determined empirically on a per-pathogen, per-assay basis, providing
no general design criterion.

Here, a formal probabilistic model of reference-based $w$-mer classification
is developed and three theorems are established. A minimum sequencing depth
bound $T^{*}(p,\mu,w,L,\delta)$ is derived as an explicit closed-form
function of pathogen abundance $p$, per-base genomic divergence $\mu$,
classifier window length $w$, read length $L$, and target false-negative
rate $\delta$. The derivation rests on the exact probability $q(w,L,\mu)$
that a single read from a divergent pathogen contains at least one
classifiable $w$-mer, computed via a linear recurrence on the run-length
distribution of Bernoulli trials. The critical divergence $\mu_c$ --- the
maximum divergence at which detection can be guaranteed within a practical
sequencing budget --- is proved unique and computable by bisection on the
exact recurrence, providing a quantitative boundary between classifiable and
effectively novel pathogens. The optimal classifier window length
$w^{*}=\lceil\log_4(Q\,n_{\mathrm{avg}}/\alpha)\rceil$ is shown to minimize
required depth subject to a false-positive rate constraint, and the depth
penalty for a longer window grows rapidly with divergence: a window 10
bases longer than $w^{*}$ requires $2.3\times$ more reads at $\mu=0.10$ and
$3.9\times$ more at $\mu_c$. A methodologically significant finding is that
the Poisson approximation commonly used to estimate classifiable-read
probability overestimates $q(w,L,\mu)$ by 72\% at $\mu=0.12$ alone, with the
overestimate exceeding 100\% as $\mu$ approaches $\mu_c$ from below, making
the exact recurrence essential for conservative depth planning. The same
approximation also overestimates the critical divergence itself ($0.199$
versus the exact $0.1349$ at $T_{\max}=10^5$). All results are validated by
simulation on real SARS-CoV-2 (Wuhan reference), Influenza A H3N2 HA, and
H1N1 HA genomes across biologically motivated divergence scenarios, with
theory--empirical gaps of at most $0.062$ across all experiments ($0.033$ on
the real genomes). The framework extends Eidenschink Brodersen et
al.\ (2020) by introducing the divergence dimension absent from their model
and provides the first principled basis for sequencing depth selection in
mNGS assay design when pathogen identity and divergence are uncertain.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

\subsection{The Clinical Problem}

Sepsis --- the life-threatening dysregulation of the immune response
triggered by infection --- kills an estimated 11 million people per year
worldwide~\cite{who2024}. Its fatality rate rises by roughly 7\% for every
hour that passes before the correct treatment begins~\cite{kumar2006}, which
places diagnostic speed at the center of patient outcomes. The standard
clinical workflow for identifying the causative pathogen is microbiological
culture: a sample of blood, cerebrospinal fluid, or other tissue is
incubated on a growth medium until the pathogen reproduces in sufficient
quantity to be identified. This process typically requires 24--72 hours, by
which time critical treatment windows may already have closed.

Metagenomic next-generation sequencing (mNGS) offers a fundamentally
different approach. Rather than culturing the organism, mNGS sequences all
DNA molecules in a patient sample simultaneously --- human, bacterial,
viral, and fungal --- and computationally separates them by comparison to a
reference database. This process can be completed in under six
hours~\cite{wilson2019}. Clinical studies have demonstrated mNGS's ability
to detect pathogens missed by conventional testing, particularly in
immunocompromised patients and in cases of neurological infection where
standard cultures have low sensitivity~\cite{wilson2019}. The technology is
increasingly deployed for exactly the most difficult diagnostic questions:
rare organisms, unusual presentations, and suspected but unconfirmed
pathogens.

The central vulnerability of mNGS is also the context in which it is most
needed. When a pathogen has diverged --- through natural mutation, antigenic
drift, or in an early-pandemic scenario --- from any sequence currently in
the reference database, the classifier may fail to recognize its reads. The
pathogen is present in the sample; its DNA is being sequenced; but the
algorithm does not flag it. This is not a failure of the sequencing machine.
It is a failure of the matching logic, and it is principled and predictable:
the classifier requires exact string matches at the nucleotide level, and
sufficiently diverged sequences will not produce them.

The question this paper addresses is: given a known or estimated level of
divergence, how much sequencing is needed to guarantee detection? This
question has no existing answer in the literature.

\subsection{How mNGS Classification Works: the $k$-mer Pipeline}

To understand why divergence causes failure and how to quantify it, we first
need to understand the classification mechanism precisely.

A DNA genome is a string over the four-letter alphabet $\{A,T,G,C\}$. A
sequencing machine does not read the genome all at once; instead, it
randomly samples millions of short fragments, called reads, each typically
75--300 nucleotides (base pairs, bp) in length. The collection of all reads
from one experiment is a random coverage of the original genome, weighted by
the molecular abundance of each species in the sample.

A $w$-mer (also called a $k$-mer) is a contiguous substring of length $w$.
From a read of length $L$, one can extract $L-w+1$ overlapping $w$-mers by
sliding a window of width $w$ across the read one position at a time. The
classifier used in tools such as Kraken2~\cite{wood2019} maintains a hash
table mapping every $w$-mer present in any reference genome to the lowest
common ancestor (LCA) of the organisms that contain it. At query time, each
read is decomposed into its $L-w+1$ $w$-mers. For each $w$-mer that exists
in the hash table, the corresponding LCA annotation is recorded. The read is
then assigned a species classification based on the majority LCA among its
$w$-mers, using a vote-weighted scheme.

The critical property of this design is that all matches are exact. A
$w$-mer from the patient sample must be character-for-character identical to
a $w$-mer in the database to be counted as a match. There is no fuzzy
matching, no edit-distance tolerance, no alignment step. This makes the
classifier extremely fast --- hash table lookups are $O(1)$ --- and
extremely specific --- only true exact matches contribute --- but it is also
the source of the divergence problem.

\subsection{Why Divergence Causes Detection Failure}

Pathogens mutate continuously. A substitution mutation is a point change:
one nucleotide is replaced by a different one. If the pathogen's genome has
diverged from the nearest reference at per-base rate $\mu$ (meaning each
position has independently mutated with probability $\mu$), then any
specific $w$-mer from the patient's sample survives as an exact match to the
reference only if all $w$ positions within it are unmutated. Under the
independent substitution model, the probability of this event is:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wmer}
  \Pr(w\text{-mer is exact match}) = (1-\mu)^{w}.
\end{equation}
This probability decays exponentially with $w$. For $\mu=0.05$ (5\%
divergence) and $w=24$: $(0.95)^{24}\approx 0.292$. About 71\% of all
$w$-mers from this pathogen would fail to match, meaning the classifier
receives only a thinned signal. At $\mu=0.15$ and $w=24$:
$(0.85)^{24}\approx 0.020$. Now 98\% of $w$-mers are invisible to the
classifier.

A read of length $L$ contains $L-w+1$ such windows. The read is detectable
--- in the sense that it contributes to species classification --- only if
at least one of those windows is an exact match. The probability of this
event is not simply $(L-w+1)(1-\mu)^{w}$, however, because adjacent windows
overlap: window $i$ covers positions $i$ through $i+w-1$ and window $i+1$
covers positions $i+1$ through $i+w$. They share $w-1$ positions. Whether
window $i$ is a match and whether window $i+1$ is a match are therefore
strongly positively correlated: if the shared $w-1$ positions all match (a
likely event when $\mu$ is small), both windows succeed or fail together.
This correlation makes a naive independence calculation wrong, and it is why
a standard approximation used in the field --- the Poisson approximation ---
substantially overestimates the informative-read probability once $\mu$ is
moderate ($\mu\gtrsim 0.10$ for the parameters studied here). Section~2
introduces the exact function $q(w,L,\mu)$ that handles this correlation
correctly via a linear recurrence on the longest-run distribution of
Bernoulli trials.

\subsection{The Gap in the Existing Theory}

The question of how much sequencing depth is needed to detect a given
pathogen is not new, but it has only been addressed incompletely.

Eidenschink Brodersen et al.~\cite{brodersen2020} derive a minimum
sequencing depth formula using a Bernoulli probability model. Their result
correctly captures the effect of abundance $p$ and genome length $N$: lower
abundance and shorter genomes require deeper sequencing to guarantee
detection. However, their model treats every read from the pathogen as
equally informative regardless of how diverged the pathogen is from the
reference. In their framework, the probability that a pathogen read is
classifiable is always 1; divergence does not enter the calculation. This is
a reasonable approximation for strains that are nearly identical to a
reference entry, but it fails completely for the cases of greatest clinical
interest: a novel variant, a cross-species spillover, or a pathogen with no
close relative in the database.

On the mathematical side, the statistics of $k$-mers under mutation have
been studied~\cite{blanca2022,hera2023}, but with a different goal:
estimating the mutation rate $\mu$ from an observed $k$-mer profile, not
guaranteeing detection under a known or assumed $\mu$. The Lander--Waterman
model~\cite{lander1988} addresses sequencing depth from a physical coverage
standpoint: how many reads does it take to cover every position in a genome
at least once? This answers a different question (assembly completeness vs.\
classifier detection) and does not incorporate the $k$-mer matching step.
Kraken2 and related tools come with extensive empirical benchmarks of
detection sensitivity across organisms and sequencing
depths~\cite{wood2019,lu2022}, but these are per-organism measurements, not
closed-form formulas. No single formula exists that expresses minimum depth
as a function of abundance, divergence, classifier window length, read
length, and target error probability simultaneously.

That is the gap this paper fills.

\subsection{Contributions of This Work}

The present paper makes the following contributions.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{The informative-read probability $q(w,L,\mu)$.} The function
  $q(w,L,\mu)$ is introduced as the probability that a single read of length
  $L$ from a pathogen with per-base divergence $\mu$ contains at least one
  $w$-mer exactly matching the reference. This is equivalent to the
  probability that a sequence of $L$ Bernoulli$(1-\mu)$ trials contains at
  least one run of $w$ consecutive successes, a classical combinatorial
  problem whose solution via a linear recurrence of order $w$ is derived in
  Definition~2.3. The Poisson approximation used in prior work is shown to
  overestimate $q(w,L,\mu)$ by 72\% at $\mu=0.12$ alone, with the
  overestimate exceeding 100\% as $\mu$ approaches $\mu_c$ from below ---
  precisely the regime of greatest clinical relevance (Section~4.3).

  \item \textbf{Theorem 1: Minimum sequencing depth.} The minimum number of
  reads guaranteeing $\delta$-detection of a pathogen at abundance $p$ with
  divergence $\mu$ under $w$-mer classification with read length $L$ is
  proved to be:
  \[
    T^{*} = \left\lceil \frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{-\ln\bigl(1-p\cdot q(w,L,\mu)\bigr)}\right\rceil .
  \]
  The proof proceeds via a direct geometric probability argument
  (Section~3.1). A Monotonicity Corollary establishes that $T^{*}$ is
  strictly increasing in both $\mu$ and $w$, with immediate design
  implications: more divergent pathogens and longer classifier windows both
  require deeper sequencing.

  \item \textbf{Theorem 2: Critical divergence $\mu_c$.} The critical
  divergence $\mu_c$ --- the maximum divergence at which detection can be
  guaranteed within a practical sequencing budget $T_{\max}$ --- is defined
  and proved to be the unique solution to
  $p\cdot q(w,L,\mu_c)=1-\delta^{1/T_{\max}}\;(\approx\ln(1/\delta)/T_{\max})$,
  with uniqueness established via the Intermediate Value Theorem
  (Section~3.2). This provides the first formal definition of the boundary
  between classifiable and effectively novel pathogens. Notably, $\mu_c$
  depends on all detection parameters, and increasing read length $L$ raises
  it --- providing a formal basis for the practical preference for long-read
  platforms in novel-pathogen detection.

  \item \textbf{Theorem 3: Optimal classifier window $w^{*}$.} An inherent
  sensitivity--specificity tradeoff governs the choice of $w$: smaller $w$
  increases $q(w,L,\mu)$ (greater sensitivity to diverged pathogens) but
  raises the probability of spurious matches from background DNA (reduced
  specificity). The optimal $w$ minimizing $T^{*}$ subject to a
  false-positive rate constraint $\alpha$ is proved to be:
  \[
    w^{*}=\left\lceil \log_4\!\left(\frac{Q\cdot n_{\mathrm{avg}}}{\alpha}\right)\right\rceil,
  \]
  and that the depth penalty for over-specifying $w$ beyond $w^{*}$ grows
  rapidly with divergence (Section~3.3). For standard database and
  specificity parameters, this yields $w^{*}=24$ --- the empirical value
  used by SecureDNA and similar systems, here receiving its first
  theoretical justification.

  \item \textbf{Empirical validation on real genomes.} All three theorems
  are validated by simulation on the SARS-CoV-2 Wuhan reference genome
  (29,903 bp), Influenza A H3N2 HA segment (1,737 bp), and Influenza A H1N1
  HA segment (1,752 bp), with biologically motivated divergence values
  spanning Tier 1 through Tier 3. The maximum gap between theoretical
  prediction and empirical detection rate is $0.033$ across the real-genome
  scenarios (Section~4.5) and $0.062$ across all experiments
  (Section~4.3).
\end{enumerate}

Section~2 establishes the formal model, including the exact definition of

\section{Model and Definitions}

The following model formalises a clinical mNGS experiment. A patient sample
contains genomic material from host and microbial sources. A pathogen of
interest $P$ is present at relative abundance $p\in(0,1)$: a fraction $p$ of
all sequenced reads originate from $P$. The pathogen genome has length $N$
base pairs. The reference database $\mathcal{D}$ contains $Q$ sequences of
average length $n_{\mathrm{avg}}$ base pairs; the nearest reference to $P$
in $\mathcal{D}$ diverges from the true genome at per-base rate $\mu$ under
an independent substitution model (i.e., each base independently disagrees
with the reference with probability $\mu$).

\begin{definition}[Divergence tier]\label{def:tier}
Let $\mu\in[0,1]$ denote the per-base divergence between a target pathogen
genome and its nearest reference in $\mathcal{D}$.
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Tier 1 (Known):} $\mu=0$. An exact reference exists in
  $\mathcal{D}$.
  \item \textbf{Tier 2 (Divergent--detectable):} $0<\mu\le\mu_c$, where
  $\mu_c$ is the critical divergence defined in Theorem~3.3. A
  reference-based $w$-mer classifier can still guarantee $\delta$-detection
  within a practical sequencing budget $T_{\max}$.
  \item \textbf{Tier 3 (Effectively novel):} $\mu>\mu_c$. No
  reference-based $w$-mer classifier guarantees detection within budget
  $T_{\max}$. Detection requires reference-free methods (de novo assembly,
  anomaly detection); this regime is out of scope for the present work.
\end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{remark}\label{rem:muc-dep}
$\mu_c$ is not a fixed biological constant: it depends on $w$, $L$, $p$,
$\delta$, and $T_{\max}$. In particular, increasing read length $L$ raises
$\mu_c$ for all other fixed parameters, providing a formal rationale for the
practical preference for long-read platforms in novel-pathogen detection.
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Informative read]\label{def:informative}
Let all reads have fixed length $L$ base pairs. A read $r$ originating from
pathogen $P$ with divergence $\mu$ is \emph{informative} for a $w$-mer
classifier if it contains at least one substring of length $w$ that is an
exact match to the corresponding window in the reference sequence.
\end{definition}

Under the independent-substitution model, each base of $r$ independently
agrees with the reference with probability $1-\mu$. Define
$P_n=P_n(w,1-\mu)$ as the probability that a sequence of $n$ independent
Bernoulli$(1-\mu)$ trials contains \emph{no} run of $w$ consecutive
successes. $P_n$ satisfies the linear recurrence
\begin{equation}\label{eq:recurrence}
  P_n=\sum_{j=0}^{\min(n-1,\,w-1)}(1-\mu)^{j}\,\mu\,P_{n-j-1},\qquad n\ge 1,
\end{equation}
with $P_0=1$. The exact informative probability is then
\begin{equation}\label{eq:qexact}
  q(w,L,\mu)=1-P_L(w,1-\mu).
\end{equation}
The Poisson approximation --- which treats the $L-w+1$ windows as
independent, and is used hereafter only for closed-form comparison
(Section~5.2 shows that it is unreliable here) --- is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:poisson}
  q(w,L,\mu)\approx 1-\exp\!\bigl(-(L-w+1)(1-\mu)^{w}\bigr).
\end{equation}

\begin{remark}[Derivation of recurrence \eqref{eq:recurrence}]\label{rem:deriv}
Condition on the position of the first failure (mismatch, probability
$\mu$) in the Bernoulli sequence. If the first failure occurs at position
$j+1$ for $j\in\{0,\dots,w-1\}$, the preceding $j$ bases are successes, no
run of length $w$ has yet been completed, and the problem reduces to the
remaining $n-j-1$ trials. Summing over all such $j$ yields
\eqref{eq:recurrence}. The recurrence has order $w$ and can be evaluated in
$O(wL)$ time, making it computationally tractable for the parameter ranges
of interest ($w\le 40$, $L\le 300$).
\end{remark}

\begin{definition}[Detection event]\label{def:detection}
Given a sequencing run of $T$ reads, let $E$ denote the event that at least
one informative pathogen read is produced. The false-negative event is
$\overline{E}$: no informative pathogen read appears. The classifier is said
to guarantee $\delta$-detection if $\Pr(\overline{E})\le\delta$.
\end{definition}

\section{Main Results}

\subsection{Minimum Sequencing Depth}

\begin{theorem}[Minimum sequencing depth]\label{thm:depth}
Under the model of Definitions~2.1--2.5, the minimum sequencing depth
guaranteeing $\delta$-detection is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:tstar}
  T^{*}(p,\mu,w,L,\delta)=\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{-\ln\bigl(1-p\cdot q(w,L,\mu)\bigr)}\right\rceil
  \;\le\;\left\lceil\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{p\cdot q(w,L,\mu)}\right\rceil,
\end{equation}
where the right-hand expression is the small-$pq$ approximation, an upper
bound on $T^{*}$ that is tight to within a factor of 2 (up to rounding)
whenever $p\cdot q(w,L,\mu)\le 0.5$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Each read is independently a pathogen read with probability $p$, and each
pathogen read is independently informative with probability
$q=q(w,L,\mu)$ (Definition~2.3). Hence each read is an informative pathogen
read with probability $pq$, independently across reads. The false-negative
event requires all $T$ reads to be non-informative pathogen reads:
\[
  \Pr(\overline{E})=(1-pq)^{T}.
\]
Setting $\Pr(\overline{E})\le\delta$ and solving:
\[
  (1-pq)^{T}\le\delta\iff T\ln(1-pq)\le\ln\delta\iff T\ge\frac{\ln(1/\delta)}{-\ln(1-pq)}.
\]
Taking the ceiling yields the exact minimum integer $T^{*}$. Since
$-\ln(1-x)\ge x$ for $x\in[0,1)$ (with equality only at $x=0$), we have
$-\ln(1-pq)\ge pq$, hence $\ln(1/\delta)/(-\ln(1-pq))\le\ln(1/\delta)/(pq)$,
which gives the right-hand bound in \eqref{eq:tstar}. For $x\le 1/2$,
$-\ln(1-x)\le 2x$, so whenever $pq\le 1/2$ the right-hand expression exceeds
the exact value by at most a factor of 2 (up to rounding).
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[Monotonicity]\label{cor:mono}
$T^{*}$ is strictly increasing in $\mu$ and strictly increasing in $w$, for
all $\mu\in(0,1)$, $w\ge1$, $L>w$.
\end{corollary}

\begin{proof}
$q(w,L,\mu)=1-P_L(w,1-\mu)$ is strictly decreasing in $\mu$: larger
divergence makes each base more likely to mismatch, reducing the probability
of any run of $w$ consecutive matches. Similarly, $q(w,L,\mu)$ is strictly
decreasing in $w$: a longer run is harder to achieve for any fixed $\mu>0$.
Since $T^{*}$ is a strictly decreasing function of $q>0$, strict monotonicity
in both $\mu$ and $w$ follows.
\end{proof}

\subsection{Critical Divergence}

\begin{theorem}[Critical divergence $\mu_c$]\label{thm:muc}
Fix $w,L,p,\delta$ and an integer depth ceiling $T_{\max}$ with
$p>1-\delta^{1/T_{\max}}$ (so that a perfectly matching pathogen is
detectable within $T_{\max}$). Let
\[
  \theta=\frac{1-\delta^{1/T_{\max}}}{p}\in(0,1),
\]
which agrees with $\ln(1/\delta)/(pT_{\max})$ to relative error
$\ln(1/\delta)/(2T_{\max})$. Define
$\mu_c=\sup\{\mu\in[0,1]:T^{*}(p,\mu,w,L,\delta)\le T_{\max}\}$. Then
$\mu_c$ is the unique solution of
\begin{equation}\label{eq:muc}
  q(w,L,\mu_c)=\theta,
\end{equation}
where $q$ is the exact informative-read probability of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:qexact}, and $T^{*}\le T_{\max}$ if and only if $\mu\le\mu_c$.
For $\mu>\mu_c$, no reference-based $w$-mer classifier achieves
$\delta$-detection within $T_{\max}$ reads (Tier 3, Definition~2.1).
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
Since $T_{\max}$ is an integer, $T^{*}\le T_{\max}$ iff
$\ln(1/\delta)/(-\ln(1-pq))\le T_{\max}$ iff $1-pq\le\delta^{1/T_{\max}}$
iff $q\ge\theta$. By Eq.~\eqref{eq:recurrence}, $q(w,L,\cdot)$ is a
polynomial in $\mu$, hence continuous on $[0,1]$, with $q(w,L,0)=1>\theta$
and $q(w,L,1)=0<\theta$. It is strictly decreasing on $(0,1)$ by
Corollary~3.2, hence strictly decreasing on $[0,1]$ by continuity. The
intermediate value theorem and strict monotonicity give a unique $\mu_c$
with $q(w,L,\mu_c)=\theta$, and $q\ge\theta$ exactly when $\mu\le\mu_c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Computing $\mu_c$]\label{rem:compmuc}
Equation~\eqref{eq:muc} is solved by bisection, each evaluation of $q$
costing $O(wL)$ via the recurrence \eqref{eq:recurrence}. Substituting the
Poisson approximation \eqref{eq:poisson} gives the closed form
\[
  \mu_c^{\mathrm{Pois}}=1-\left(\frac{-\ln(1-\theta)}{L-w+1}\right)^{1/w}.
\]
Because \eqref{eq:poisson} overestimates $q$ (Section~5.2),
$\mu_c^{\mathrm{Pois}}$ overestimates $\mu_c$. For the parameters of
Section~4 ($p=10^{-4}$, $w=24$, $L=150$, $\delta=0.01$, $T_{\max}=10^5$) the
exact value is $\mu_c=0.1349$ while the closed form gives $0.199$, about $48\%$
too high. All values of $\mu_c$ reported in this paper are exact. Using the
closed form would misclassify $\mu=0.15$ and $\mu=0.18$ as Tier~2, although
they require $130{,}987$ and $243{,}553$ reads (Table~1).
\end{remark}

\subsection{Optimal Classifier Window Length}

\begin{theorem}[Optimal $w$-mer length $w^{*}$]\label{thm:wstar}
Let $\alpha\in(0,1)$ be a target false-positive rate, where a false positive
arises when a background (non-pathogen) read contains a $w$-mer matching the
pathogen reference. Modeling database $w$-mer content as uniformly
distributed over $4^{w}$ possible sequences, the false-positive probability
for a single $w$-mer query is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:pfp}
  P_{\mathrm{FP}}(w)\approx\frac{Q\cdot n_{\mathrm{avg}}}{4^{w}}.
\end{equation}
The optimal window length minimizing $T^{*}$ subject to
$P_{\mathrm{FP}}(w)\le\alpha$ is
\begin{equation}\label{eq:wstar}
  w^{*}=\left\lceil\log_4\!\left(\frac{Q\cdot n_{\mathrm{avg}}}{\alpha}\right)\right\rceil.
\end{equation}
For $\mu>0$, using any $w>w^{*}$ strictly increases required sequencing depth
without improving false-positive performance.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
By Corollary~3.2, $T^{*}(w)$ is strictly increasing in $w$. Therefore the
minimum of $T^{*}$ over the feasible set
$\mathcal{W}=\{w\in\mathbb{Z}^{+}:P_{\mathrm{FP}}(w)\le\alpha\}$ is attained
at $w^{\dagger}=\min\mathcal{W}$. The constraint $P_{\mathrm{FP}}(w)\le\alpha$
with \eqref{eq:pfp} becomes
\[
  4^{w}\ge\frac{Q\cdot n_{\mathrm{avg}}}{\alpha}\iff w\ge\log_4\!\left(\frac{Q\cdot n_{\mathrm{avg}}}{\alpha}\right),
\]
so $w^{\dagger}=\lceil\log_4(Q\cdot n_{\mathrm{avg}}/\alpha)\rceil=w^{*}$.

The cost of over-specification: for $w=w^{*}+\Delta$ with $\Delta\ge1$,
strict monotonicity in $w$ gives $T^{*}(w^{*}+\Delta)>T^{*}(w^{*})$ for every
$\mu>0$. When $pq\ll1$ the ratio is approximately
$q(w^{*},L,\mu)/q(w^{*}+\Delta,L,\mu)$; for $w^{*}=24$, $\Delta=10$ this
ratio is $2.3$ at $\mu=0.10$, $3.9$ at $\mu_c=0.1349$, and $9.7$ at
$\mu=0.20$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[Tradeoff sharpening under divergence]\label{rem:tradeoff}
At $\mu=0$, $q(w,L,0)=1$ for all $w\le L$, so $T^{*}$ is independent of $w$
and any $w\ge w^{*}$ is acceptable. For $\mu>0$, $T^{*}$ grows strictly in
$w$ beyond $w^{*}$, and the numerical penalty grows rapidly with $\mu$
(Section~3.3 and Table~2). This is the central design implication: as
genomic divergence increases, the penalty for over-specifying $w$ grows,
making the principled choice of $w=w^{*}$ progressively more consequential.
\end{remark}

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Simulation Methodology}

All experiments are conducted using a Python simulator developed specifically
to validate Theorems~1--3. The simulator implements the following pipeline
for each parameter configuration $(p,\mu,w,L,T,n_{\text{trials}})$:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Pathogen genome generation.} For each independent trial, a
  fresh diverged pathogen genome is generated by applying independent
  substitution mutations at per-base rate $\mu$ to the reference sequence.
  Each base is independently replaced by a uniformly random alternative base
  with probability $\mu$, and left unchanged with probability $1-\mu$. Using
  a fresh genome per trial ensures that the empirical detection rate
  averages over the distribution of possible mutated sequences rather than
  fixing one realization. All pathogen reads within a trial are drawn from
  this one genome (see Section~5.4).

  \item \textbf{Read sampling.} Each of the $T$ reads is drawn by: (a)
  sampling a Bernoulli$(p)$ random variable to decide whether the read
  originates from the pathogen or the background; (b) if it is a pathogen
  read, sampling a uniformly random start position and extracting a
  length-$L$ substring from the diverged pathogen genome.

  \item \textbf{Informative-read test.} For each pathogen read, we slide a
  window of length $w$ across all $L-w+1$ positions and check whether any
  window in the read is an exact character-for-character match to the
  corresponding window in the reference sequence at the same start position.
  If at least one window matches, the read is declared informative and
  detection is registered for that trial. The trial terminates early upon
  the first informative read, matching the definition of $\delta$-detection.

  \item \textbf{Detection rate computation.} The empirical detection rate
  for a given parameter set is the fraction of $n_{\text{trials}}$
  independent trials in which at least one informative read was observed.
\end{enumerate}
All experiments use $\delta=0.01$, $L=150$ bp, $w=24$, and $p=10^{-4}$; the
number of trials is stated in each table caption. The depth ceiling is
$T_{\max}=10^5$ reads throughout, which is also the experimental depth $T$;
$\mu_c$ is evaluated at this $T_{\max}$ using the exact recurrence, giving
$\mu_c=0.1349$. The simulator code and reference genomes are available at
\url{https://github.com/DevanshMalhotra17/mNGS-depth-guarantees}.

\subsection{Theoretical Values}

For each parameter set, four quantities are derived from the theory:
\begin{itemize}
  \item $q_{\mathrm{exact}}$: the exact informative-read probability from the
  linear recurrence (Eq.~\eqref{eq:recurrence}).
  \item $q_{\mathrm{Poisson}}$: the Poisson approximation
  (Eq.~\eqref{eq:poisson}), for comparison.
  \item $T^{*}$: the minimum depth from Theorem~3.1.
  \item $D_{\mathrm{theory}}$: the predicted detection probability at the
  experimental depth $T$, computed as $1-(1-p\cdot q_{\mathrm{exact}})^{T}$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion}

\subsection{A Formula for Assay Design}

The central result of this paper --- Equation~\eqref{eq:tstar} --- provides
clinical laboratorians and computational biologists with a design tool that
has not previously existed: a formula that translates assay design choices
directly into a detection guarantee.

Consider the practical use case. A hospital laboratory is validating an mNGS
protocol for sepsis workup. The protocol uses Illumina short reads
($L=150$ bp) and Kraken2 with $w=24$. The laboratory wants to guarantee
detection of a mildly diverged Tier 2 pathogen ($\mu=0.02$, about an order of
magnitude more divergent than the SARS-CoV-2 Omicron scenario in Table~3)
present at abundance $p\ge10^{-6}$ (one pathogen molecule per million,
corresponding to a very low pathogen load) with false-negative rate
$\delta\le0.01$. Plugging into Equation~\eqref{eq:tstar}:
$T^{*}\approx4.6\times10^{6}$ reads. Current short-read platforms routinely
produce this output within a single sequencing run. Without this formula,
the laboratory would have had to run empirical spike-in experiments across
many pathogen strains to establish empirical sensitivity, with no guarantee
that the tested strains covered the relevant divergence space.

The phase diagram implied by Theorem~3.1 --- $T^{*}$ as a joint function of
$p$ and $\mu$ at fixed $w$, $L$, $\delta$ --- provides an even more
actionable tool. For a suspected pathogen with an estimated abundance range
and a known or bounded divergence from the nearest reference, the laboratory
can read off the minimum depth directly.

\subsection{The Failure of the Poisson Approximation}

The gap between $q_{\mathrm{exact}}$ and $q_{\mathrm{Poisson}}$ documented
in Table~1 is not merely a mathematical curiosity; it has concrete
consequences for sequencing depth estimation.

At $\mu=0.12$, the Poisson approximation gives
$q_{\mathrm{Poisson}}\approx0.997$, yielding $T^{*}\approx46{,}200$ reads ---
nearly the same as for an exact reference match. The exact recurrence gives
$q_{\mathrm{exact}}=0.580$, yielding $T^{*}\approx79{,}400$ reads --- 72\%
more. A laboratory using the Poisson approximation to plan its sequencing
depth would be under-sequencing by a factor of 1.72 for pathogens at this
level of divergence. The problem worsens closer to the detection boundary:
within Tier 2 itself, the overestimate ratio
$q_{\mathrm{Poisson}}/q_{\mathrm{exact}}$ grows from 1.72 at $\mu=0.12$ to
1.98 at $\mu=0.13$ and exceeds 2.1 by $\mu=0.134$, immediately below
$\mu_c=0.1349$ --- meaning the approximation is least reliable exactly where
the detection guarantee is most marginal and most consequential to get
right.

The overestimate is negligible at small divergence (ratio $1.00$ at
$\mu\le0.05$) and grows with $\mu$: $1.34$ at $\mu=0.10$, $1.72$ at $0.12$,
and $3.8$ at $0.20$. It does not require $\lambda$ to be large; at
$\mu=0.30$, $\lambda\approx0.024$ yet the ratio is still $3.2$. The cause is
clumping, as described in Section~4.3: the Poisson count treats every
matching window as an independent chance, but windows occur in overlapping
clusters whose mean size is roughly $1/\mu$. Counting clusters rather than
windows, i.e.\ replacing $\lambda$ by $\lambda\mu$ (the standard
Poisson-clumping heuristic for success runs), gives $q\approx0.113$ against
an exact $0.119$ at $\mu=0.20$, and $0.00727$ against $0.00742$ at
$\mu=0.30$. This heuristic is a rare-event approximation and is poor at
small $\mu$ ($0.79$ versus $0.9999$ at $\mu=0.02$), so the exact recurrence
remains the recommended tool across the full range.

The consequences extend to $\mu_c$. Evaluated with the Poisson
approximation, the critical divergence is $0.199$ rather than $0.1349$
(Remark~3.1); a laboratory using it would treat a pathogen at $\mu=0.18$ as
detectable at $10^5$ reads when the exact requirement is $243{,}553$.

\subsection{The Read-Length Lever}

A consequence of Theorem~3.3 that deserves emphasis is the role of read
length $L$ in determining $\mu_c$. As $L$ increases (with $w$ and $\mu$
fixed), the number of candidate windows per read ($L-w+1$) increases, raising
$q$ and therefore lowering $T^{*}$ and raising $\mu_c$. This provides a
formal quantitative justification for a well-known empirical preference:
long-read sequencing platforms (Oxford Nanopore, PacBio) are systematically
preferred for novel pathogen detection precisely because they provide more
windows per read, making it more likely that at least one survives
divergence. The advantage is particularly pronounced near the Tier 2/Tier 3
boundary, where small improvements in $q$ translate to large reductions in
required depth.

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Independent substitution model.} The analysis assumes that each
base position mutates independently with probability $\mu$. Real pathogen
evolution involves insertions, deletions, recombination, and
context-dependent substitution rates. Insertions and deletions are
particularly disruptive to $w$-mer matching because they shift the reading
frame, breaking all downstream windows. A natural extension of this work
would incorporate an insertion-deletion rate alongside the substitution
rate, using a more general sequence evolution model (e.g., the Jukes--Cantor
or Kimura models).

\paragraph{Read independence.} Theorem~3.1 treats pathogen reads as
independent, whereas in a single trial (and in a real sample) all pathogen
reads derive from one mutated genome, so reads overlap and share mutations.
Detection then depends on the fraction $f$ of read positions that are
informative in that particular genome, and since $1-(1-pf)^{T}$ is concave in
$f$, Jensen's inequality gives
$\E\bigl[1-(1-pf)^{T}\bigr]\le1-(1-p\,\E[f])^{T}$, with $\E[f]=q$:
expected empirical detection is at most the theoretical value, with a gap
that shrinks as the genome length grows. Computing this expectation directly
for $T=10^{5}$ gives $0.818$ at $\mu=0.18$ and $0.654$ at $\mu=0.20$ for a
5,000 bp genome (theory: $0.849$ and $0.695$; Table~1 empirical: $0.797$ and
$0.633$, each within about one standard error of the expectation), and
$0.689$ at $\mu=0.20$ for a 29,903 bp genome (theory $0.695$; Table~3
empirical $0.663$). The effect is therefore largest for short genomes and
small for SARS-CoV-2-length genomes.

\paragraph{Non-uniform genome coverage.} Reads are modeled as drawn uniformly
from the pathogen genome. Real sequencing exhibits GC-dependent amplification
bias and adapter-related coverage non-uniformities. In practice this means
certain genomic regions are sequenced much more deeply than others, and the
effective abundance $p$ for those regions differs from the global average.
The bound $T^{*}$ is conservative in regions of high coverage and may be
insufficient in regions of low coverage.

\paragraph{Sequencing errors.} Sequencing machines produce errors ---
incorrect base calls at a rate of approximately 0.1--1\% per base for
short-read platforms. A sequencing error on a position that would otherwise
have been a match converts it to a mismatch, effectively increasing the
apparent divergence. This can be incorporated by replacing $\mu$ with an
effective divergence $\mu_{\mathrm{eff}}=\mu+e(1-\mu)\approx\mu+e$, where
$e$ is the per-base error rate. For Illumina platforms ($e\approx0.001$),
this correction is negligible for $\mu\ge0.01$ but becomes non-trivial near
$\mu=0$.

\paragraph{Tier 3 scope.} The present work is confined to Tier 2: pathogens
for which a reference-based approach can succeed. Tier 3 (effectively novel)
pathogens require reference-free detection --- de novo assembly or anomaly
detection in read embeddings --- and their sample-complexity theory is an
open problem.

\subsection{Future Directions}

Several extensions follow naturally from the framework established here.

\paragraph{Multi-pathogen panels.} Clinical mNGS workflows typically screen
for many potential pathogens simultaneously. A depth guarantee for a panel of
$D$ candidate pathogens requires a union bound over $D$ independent
detection problems, each with its own $(p_i,\mu_i)$ pair, leading to a
panel-wide depth requirement
$T^{*}_{\mathrm{panel}}\approx\max_i T^{*}(p_i,\mu_i,w,L,\delta/D)$ by the
Bonferroni correction. The optimal $w^{*}$ for a panel depends on the joint
distribution of $(p_i,\mu_i)$ across pathogens and is an interesting
combinatorial optimization problem.

\paragraph{Level 2 model.} The Level 1 model presented here admits a natural
Level 2 extension that incorporates the classifier's database structure:
rather than treating any $w$-mer match as a detection event, one can weight
matches by their LCA specificity (as in Kraken2's vote-weighted scheme) and
derive an expected classification confidence score rather than a binary
detection indicator. This would connect the depth bound to the classifier's
taxonomic resolution rather than just its sensitivity.

\paragraph{Optimal sequencing platform selection.} Given a cost model for
sequencing depth as a function of platform (short-read vs.\ long-read) and a
divergence distribution for the target pathogens, Theorem~3.3 provides a
framework for choosing which platform minimizes cost subject to a detection
guarantee. This is directly actionable for hospital laboratory procurement
decisions.
